Overview

This recording captures the direct confrontation between the passenger and Joaquín Barraza, LATAM security chief, approximately 21 minutes after the luggage-counter discussion. It is one of the clearest examples in the case of a shifting-facts pattern: the removal is justified first by an alleged aggression, then by a boarding-pass issue, then by unauthorized entry, and each explanation is met with evidence that contradicts it. The decisive element is the flat refusal to provide a written explanation.

Key evidentiary fact

The strongest fact in this recording is not merely that the passenger disputes the airline’s narrative. It is the direct, on-tape admission that there is no written document to justify the action, and that the airline is refusing to cooperate because the passenger did not “collaborate” with a false accusation. This is a retaliation rationale stated aloud.

Methodology

The transcript is evaluated as a continuation of the same evidentiary chain: false aggression, shifting after-the-fact explanations, refusal to produce documentation, and institutional closure. The importance of STG_16 is that it captures the airline’s own internal logic in plain speech: the narrative keeps shifting, the paper trail does not exist, and the passenger is told no one will assist him.

Finding 1 — A third removal justification appears on tape

Barraza states: “porque ingresaste sin autorización al avión.” This is the third distinct justification for the same removal, after the earlier false-aggression allegation and the later “boarding pass” narrative. The pattern itself is evidentiary: each new explanation appears only after the previous one collapses under scrutiny, suggesting a post-hoc rationalization rather than a stable factual basis.

Finding 2 — The document does not exist, and the airline admits it

The passenger reconstructs the full chain: Barraza cites the camera, refuses to show it, and asks for a written explanation. Barraza then says, effectively, that the document does not exist. This is a critical admission: the airline claims access to the evidence but refuses to show or document it. The absence of a written explanation is not a neutral omission; it is a direct evidentiary vacuum.

  • Claim made: “I saw it on camera.”
  • Response: “I cannot show you the camera.”
  • Request: “Give me a document explaining it.”
  • Answer: no document exists.

Finding 3 — Retaliation is stated explicitly

Barraza says: “Tú no quisiste colaborar, yo no te voy a colaborar… nadie te va a colaborar.” This is not a vague suggestion but a direct statement that assistance is denied because the passenger refused to cooperate with a false accusation. That is a retaliation rationale, stated plainly and on recording.

The legal significance is substantial: the passenger is not refusing a lawful process; he is refusing to participate in a false narrative. The airline responds by turning the entire service relationship into retaliation rather than lawful process.

Finding 4 — The airline closes the channel and refuses to answer

Barraza concludes with an institutional message: “Nadie más le va a dar otra respuesta.” This echoes the same pattern seen elsewhere in the case: the passenger is not given a remedy, a written basis, or a direct response; instead, he is funneled into an internal channel that closes on him. The issue is not technical uncertainty; it is deliberate channel closure.

Finding 5 — The passenger invokes the governing law

He explicitly states: “es mi derecho… dentro de la Chilean Aviation Law.” He demands a written explanation and a certified basis for the removal. This is a legally grounded demand, not a complaint from a confused passenger. The airline’s answer is not to provide the document but to deny it exists and to deny assistance.

Summary of legal significance

STG_16 is a high-value record because it captures a direct confrontation in which the airline’s shifting removal narratives, lack of documentation, and retaliation are all stated on tape. The narrative is not a single mistake; it is a pattern of bad faith, channel closure, and procedural denial.
